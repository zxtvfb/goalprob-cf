How the forecast is made

1 · Team profile. Every team keeps its last 45 matches. From those come goals and shots on target, for and against, split by home and away. Recent matches count for more: a result fifteen matches back carries half the weight of the latest one, because a side from four months ago is a different side.

2 · Strength. Each rate is set against its league average. Goals are few and noisy, so where shots on target are on record most of the weight goes on those instead. A team with few matches is pulled toward the league average so that five games cannot produce a wild number.

2b · Ratings from closing prices. In the twenty leagues the results files cover, every match comes with the odds it closed at. Fitted across the whole league, those odds give each side a rating for quality and for how open its games are — and, because each team is measured against the opponents it actually faced, a fixture list of weak sides does not flatter it. Where both sides have a rating it carries most of the weight: tested on simulated seasons, it cut the error of the forecast almost in half.

3 · Scorelines. The two expected-goal figures fill a grid of every score from 0–0 to 8–8, with a correction for the low scores a plain Poisson grid gets wrong. Summing cells gives 1X2, both-score, over/under, correct score. The first half gets its own grid.

4 · Checked against results. The /backtest page replays the stored seasons, compares what the model said with what happened, and stores a correction for each market. Those corrections are applied here.

5 · The market. Where bookmaker prices exist, the forecast is mostly the market's own probability with the margin removed — measured against known outcomes, a liquid market is several times more accurate than any model built on results. The model fills in what the bookmakers do not quote, the half-time result above all, using the expected goals their prices imply.

6 · Value. A price is judged against the market, not against the model: the figure beside a price is what it returns per unit if the sharpest available probability is right. Plus means 1xBet is paying more than the bet is worth.

What it does not see. Team news of any kind — injuries, suspensions, line-ups, a change of manager — and the weather. Where a price exists the market has already taken those into account; where there is none, the number is built from results alone.
